Try this: Set p = 0.50, then match q to p. Entropy is maximal for this coin, but KL is zero. A perfect model can still face unpredictable data. Now push q toward 1: tails still happens half the time.
The formulas, translated
H(p) = −Σₓ p(x) log p(x)
H(p,q) = −Σₓ p(x) log q(x)
D(p ‖ q) = Σₓ p(x) log[p(x)/q(x)]

x runs over heads and tails. The sum is a weighted average: p tells you how often to count each outcome. Subtracting the first formula from the second gives KL. We treat zero-weight outcomes as contributing zero. If p(x) > 0 but q(x) = 0, cross entropy and forward KL are infinite.

KL is nonnegative even though individual terms may be negative. It is zero exactly when the distributions match. For fixed p, H(p) cannot change as you train q, so matching q to p minimizes cross entropy.

Why “bits”? And why is uniform maximally uncertain?

An outcome of probability ½ has −log₂(½) = 1 bit of surprise; probability ¼ gives 2 bits. Independent surprises add. Natural logs give nats instead: bits = nats / ln(2).

For a fixed set of K outcomes, uniform probabilities give the maximum entropy log₂ K. No outcome is favored. More formally, KL from p to the uniform distribution equals log₂ K − H(p), which cannot be negative.

For compression, a code designed for q ideally uses about −log₂ q(x) bits for x. Its average cost under reality p is cross entropy. Entropy is the best achievable average limit; KL is the wasted cost. Long-sequence coding approaches these limits.

Forward: average over reality

D(p ‖ q) = Eₓ∼p[ln p(x) − ln q(x)]

Samples arrive from both true peaks. Missing either peak makes real observations very surprising. A broad q covers both—even if it also puts probability in the valley.

Reverse: average over the model

D(q ‖ p) = Eₓ∼q[ln q(x) − ln p(x)]

Samples arrive from q. A broad q produces points in the low-probability valley. A narrow q around one peak avoids that penalty; the ignored peak receives little weight.

Try this: Compare “Cover both” with “Left peak.” The preferred model changes when you reverse the KL arguments. Then use the two fit buttons. These are tendencies under a restricted model family; if q could equal p, both objectives would choose that.
Target definition and numerical accuracy

p(x) = ½ N(−2.5, 0.7²) + ½ N(2.5, 0.7²). q(x) = N(μ, σ²). Here N denotes a normal distribution, μ its mean, and σ its standard deviation.

KL values use midpoint numerical integration in standardized coordinates from −9 to 9 with 900 intervals, plus stable log-density calculations. No artificial zero probabilities or clipped density ratios are used. “Fit forward” uses the target’s exact mean and standard deviation. “Fit reverse” uses a multi-start numerical search; this is an approximate fit, not a proof of global optimality.

Joint entropy, chain rule, and “on average”

Joint entropy H(X,Y) is uncertainty about the pair. Conditional entropy H(Y | X) is the average uncertainty about Y after seeing X. The chain rule says H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y | X). Here H(X) = H(Y) = 1 bit.

For this symmetric example, H(Y | X) = −r log₂ r − (1−r) log₂(1−r). Either observed X gives the same entropy. Mutual information I(X;Y) = H(Y) − H(Y | X) measures the average uncertainty removed.

In general, a particular observation can increase uncertainty. Suppose Y is always 0 on 90% of days, while on the other 10% it is a fair coin. Before learning the day type, P(Y=1)=0.05 and H(Y)≈0.286 bits. Learning it is a “fair coin day” raises uncertainty to 1 bit. But averaging over all day types gives H(Y | day type)=0.1 bit, which is lower. Conditioning reduces uncertainty on average.

Connect the ideas / From probability to ML

What these numbers mean in practice.

Perplexity: effective choices

Undo the logarithm: perplexity is 2H for bits or eH for nats. A fair coin has entropy 1 bit and perplexity 2. Four equally likely outcomes have entropy 2 bits and perplexity 4.

Language-model perplexity uses average cross entropy on observed next tokens: exp(−average ln q(actual token | context)). Perplexity 20 means the same average log loss as a uniform choice among 20 possibilities—not literally a vocabulary of 20 words. Compare on the same data and tokenization.

Training: reward the actual answer

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) maximizes the probability of observed data. Taking the negative log turns products into sums: NLL = −Σᵢ ln qθ(xᵢ). θ means the model’s parameters. Average NLL is empirical cross entropy.

For classification, use qθ(yᵢ | xᵢ), the probability of the correct label. Correct-label probability 0.8 costs 0.223 nats; 0.1 costs 2.303. At the population level, minimizing cross entropy minimizes forward KL because H(p) is fixed.

Why training loss isn’t enough

A model can memorize training observations and perform poorly on new data. Low training cross entropy does not guarantee low population cross entropy. Validation loss helps detect this overfitting.

Data augmentation uses meaningful label-preserving transformations to expose more variation and encourage invariance. Shifting an image might preserve its label; turning a 6 into a 9 does not. Augmentation changes the training distribution, so transformations should match the task.

Continuous entropy can be negative

Differential entropy uses a density f: h(X) = −∫ f(x) ln f(x) dx. A density can exceed 1. Uniform on an interval of width a has h = ln a: width 0.1 gives −2.303 nats.

This isn’t negative uncertainty. Differential entropy depends on units: h(cX) = h(X) + ln|c| for nonzero c. Variance measures squared spread; entropy also depends on the distribution’s shape. Equal variance does not imply equal entropy. For fixed finite variance on the real line, the Gaussian has maximum differential entropy.

A useful continuous-data caveat

KL is nonnegative for continuous distributions too and uses a density ratio. Under a common invertible change of coordinates, KL stays unchanged even though differential entropy can change. The cross-entropy identity holds when the quantities are well defined.

For continuous models, empirical NLL remains useful, but KL from discrete point-mass samples to a continuous density is generally infinite. Interpret empirical NLL as estimating population cross entropy, rather than assuming a finite KL between those incompatible representations.
